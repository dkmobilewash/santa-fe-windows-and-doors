Three things to balance on a view wall

Heat

A low solar heat gain coefficient keeps late-day sun from overheating great rooms with big west glass.

View clarity

Spectrally selective low-E coatings block much of the sun's heat while letting most visible light through, so the view stays clear rather than tinted.

UV and fading

Low-E coatings and laminated glass both cut ultraviolet light, the main driver of fading in art, textiles and wood floors.

Comparing glass before you commit

Glass coatings that look identical on a spec sheet can look quite different in a room. We bring samples you can hold up against your own view at the time of day that matters most to you, and we compare the visible light, solar heat gain and UV numbers side by side.

Because a house this size has so much glass, getting the choice right on the west side often makes the biggest difference to how the whole home feels on summer evenings.

  • West view walls: strongest solar control with high clarity
  • East and north: balanced coatings that favor insulating value
  • South: more sun allowed in where overhangs shade it in summer
  • Laminated panes where UV protection or fairway exposure matter

Las Campanas glass questions

Can we cut sunset glare without glass that looks tinted?

Yes. Spectrally selective coatings reduce heat and some glare while keeping glass close to clear. Adding stronger tint reduces glare further, but at the cost of the view, so we'll show you the difference with samples.

Will new glass protect our art collection from fading?

It will reduce the risk considerably. Low-E coatings block most UV, and laminated glass blocks nearly all of it. Visible light and heat also contribute to fading, so placement and shading still matter for valuable pieces.

Is it worth upgrading only the west-facing windows?

It can be. If heat and glare come mainly from one view wall, starting there often delivers the biggest improvement for the investment.
