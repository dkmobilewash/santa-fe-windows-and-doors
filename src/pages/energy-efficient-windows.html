Intense sun & UV

At around 7,000 feet there's less atmosphere to filter sunlight, so UV is stronger than at sea level. Low-E coatings block most UV and help reduce fading of floors, rugs, art and furniture. They also reduce unwanted summer heat gain.

Big day-to-night swings

A warm afternoon can turn into a cold night. Insulated glass with low-E coatings and gas fills, set in a well-insulated frame, helps keep that heat inside after the sun goes down.

Wind & dust

Even great glass can't help if air leaks around the frame. Careful shimming, insulation and sealing around each window, plus good weatherstripping on the sash, keep out drafts and fine dust.

Altitude

Sealed insulated glass made at low elevation can be stressed by the lower air pressure here. Glass for high-altitude homes is often built with capillary or breather tubes, or made for the elevation. We account for this when we order. Note that this can affect whether argon gas fill is available, and we'll explain your options.

Reading the label

Most windows carry an NFRC label with independently tested ratings. These are the numbers we'll walk through with you:

U-factorHow well the window keeps heat in. Lower is better for our cold winter nights.
SHGCSolar Heat Gain Coefficient: how much of the sun's heat comes through. Lower blocks more heat; higher lets in more free winter warmth.
Visible TransmittanceHow much daylight comes through. Higher means brighter rooms.
Air LeakageHow much air passes through the window itself. Lower is better for wind and dust.

Not every side of the house is the same

Santa Fe has a long tradition of passive solar design. South-facing glass can collect welcome winter sun, while west-facing windows take the hottest afternoon light in summer.

That's why we may suggest different glass for different sides of your home: for example, glass that lets in more winter sun on the south and more solar control on the west.
